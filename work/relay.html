01 — Setting

The software was beautiful. The dispatchers hated it.

The previous redesign had been won at a design award. The actual people who used the software for ten hours a day called it "the white screen." They had built a private Slack channel to share keyboard hacks for getting around the UI.

Leadership wanted "more delight." Dispatchers wanted "more information per pixel." The interesting design question was how to give both sides what they wanted with the same interface.

02 — Research

Eleven full shifts before drawing a single screen.

I spent two weeks in three different dispatch rooms. Six AM to four PM. No interviews — I just sat next to dispatchers and watched them work. I asked exactly one question, on day eight, of one person — what did they wish the software did that it didn't.

Field notes · Lot 14, Tuesday
"Most dispatchers run eleven tabs. Two are the actual app. Nine are Google Maps, weather, a spreadsheet of driver phone numbers, a CB radio web client, two Excel sheets that are older than I am, and one tab whose only job is to keep the system from logging them out."
— page 14 of 47

The actual product wasn't the screen. It was the screen plus ten browser tabs plus a phone plus a radio plus a stack of paper. The redesign's job was to absorb as much of that stack as it could.

03 — The queue

Density is a feature.

The new queue shows 28 rows on a standard 13-inch laptop without scrolling. Every column earns its place. There are no icons that aren't also keyboard shortcuts. There are no colors that don't carry meaning. Dispatchers can run an entire shift without using their mouse for anything except opening a new browser tab — and there are eleven fewer of those.

04 — Tested with the people who use it

Six dispatchers in a room every other Friday.

We ran fortnightly co-design sessions with six dispatchers from across three lots. They argued about everything. They were right about ninety percent of it. The keyboard model, the column order, the way status pills look — all of it came out of those sessions.

05 — Outcome

The Slack channel went quiet.

The private "hacks" Slack channel the dispatchers had run for years went quiet six weeks after launch. That, more than any chart, was the moment I knew it had landed.
